### Strongest point for
The affirmative side’s strongest argument is the procedural necessity of transparency for legal recourse under the Equality Act 2010 and UK GDPR. They persuasively argue that rights are functionally unenforceable if candidates are unaware an algorithm is evaluating them. Without mandatory disclosure, applicants cannot identify potential indirect algorithmic discrimination or exercise existing statutory rights to human intervention and explanation, creating an insurmountable information asymmetry.

### Strongest point against
The opposing side’s strongest argument centres on regulatory ambiguity and disproportionate compliance burdens, particularly for SMEs. They highlight that drawing a clear legal boundary between basic recruitment software and "AI" is difficult, risking legal uncertainty. Furthermore, imposing strict statutory mandates conflicts with the UK's pro-innovation approach and risks generating unhelpful boilerplate disclosures that offer little real utility to candidates.

### Which side was better supported
The **affirmative side** was slightly better supported based on the internal logic of the arguments presented. 

The affirmative established a clear, direct causal link between mandatory disclosure and the practical ability of citizens to exercise already existing statutory protections (such as anti-discrimination and data privacy rights). The opposition attempted to counter this by arguing redundancy under UK GDPR Article 22; however, this counter-argument was weakened by its own premise: an individual cannot demand an explanation or intervention under existing law if the underlying automated processing remains undisclosed. While the opposition introduced strong practical objections regarding SME burden and "gaming" the system, these points did not fully neutralise the fundamental rights-enforcement problem identified by the affirmative.

### What remains contested
- **Legal necessity vs. redundancy:** Whether current UK GDPR (Article 22) and DPA 2018 provisions already adequately compel sufficient notice in automated decision-making, or if existing rules leave an unaddressed loophole for AI-assisted hiring.
- **Definitional clarity:** How an enforceable definition of "AI" can be framed without inadvertently capturing standard hiring software (such as simple applicant tracking or keyword search tools).
- **Candidate behaviour:** Whether disclosure genuinely aids candidate fairness and accountability, or merely incentivises the "gaming" of screening tools at the expense of authentic meritocracy.
